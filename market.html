Market demand analysis in practice — learned from zero

How does an entrepreneur find the right price for a product that doesn’t exist yet? Chapter 5 measures willingness to pay (WTP) with two survey methods — the Contingent Valuation Method (CVM) and the Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (PSM) — then uses the Newton–Miller–Smith (NMS) extension to turn survey answers into a demand curve, a revenue curve and a total market forecast.

1. Willingness to pay (WTP), also called the reservation price

What it meansWTP is the maximum price a consumer will pay for a unit. The slide: “Willingness to pay (reservation prices) are the prices shown on the demand curve.”
Why it mattersIf you know how many people have WTP above each price, you know the demand curve — and therefore the best price.
On the examRead WTP off the demand curve: height of the curve at that unit.

How to measure WTP? Two methods: (1) Contingent Valuation Method (CVM) and (2) Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (PSM).

2. Contingent Valuation Method (CVM) — double-bounded

CVM: respondents are asked to indicate how much they would pay in the hypothetical scenario presented in the questionnaire. In the double-bounded version each respondent answers two sequential questions.

The entrepreneur sets three hypothetical price levels based on competitor prices and business costs:

  • \(P_0\) (original or base price, the initial bid) = 60 Baht
  • \(P_L\) (low price, the lower bid) = 40 Baht
  • \(P_H\) (high price, the higher bid) = 80 Baht
Group [Q1, Q2]Level of WTPMarket segmentMarketing strategy (slide)
[yes, yes]WTP ≥ \(P_H\) (≥ 80)Premium / LoyalThe primary target; focus early. Offer full-featured, premium or luxury versions to maximize profit per unit through price skimming.
[yes, no]\(P_0\) ≤ WTP < \(P_H\) (60 to under 80)Value-for-MoneyFocus on value for money and keep the base price level.
[no, yes]\(P_L\) ≤ WTP < \(P_0\) (40 to under 60)Price-Sensitive / PromotionBoost short-term sales with occasional discounts, discount codes, or cheaper/lower-spec (economy) versions — which also gather data on this group.
[no, no]WTP < \(P_L\) (below 40)Non-ConsumersExclude this group from all marketing communications.
Exam trap

Read the pair in the order asked. [yes, no] = yes to 60, then no to 80 → WTP between 60 and 80. [no, yes] = no to 60, then yes to 40 → WTP between 40 and 60. Nobody who says “yes” to 60 is ever asked about 40.

3. Forecasting individual and market demand from CVM

A person buys at a posted price if their WTP is at least that price. So the share who buy at each price is a cumulative sum of groups:

Groups who would buyLevel of WTPPrice% of purchases in the marketOut of 10,000 people
[yes, yes]WTP ≥ \(P_H\)8025%0.25 × 10,000 = 2,500
[yes, yes] + [yes, no]WTP ≥ \(P_0\)6050%0.50 × 10,000 = 5,000
[yes, yes] + [yes, no] + [no, yes]WTP ≥ \(P_L\)4080%0.80 × 10,000 = 8,000

The percentages are “based on calculated response proportions from the CVM survey”. Plotting (80, 0.25), (60, 0.50), (40, 0.80) gives the individual demand curve (price vs % of purchases). Assuming the target market has 10,000 people, multiplying gives the market demand curve: (80, 2,500), (60, 5,000), (40, 8,000) persons.

Implied single-group shares (our subtraction): [yes, yes] 25%, [yes, no] 50 − 25 = 25%, [no, yes] 80 − 50 = 30%, [no, no] 100 − 80 = 20% (never buy at any of the three prices).

4. Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (PSM): four questions

Instead of asking directly for WTP, the survey asks respondents to give specific monetary amounts for four price perceptions — PSM’s 4 core questions:

QuestionConsumer behaviorExample (pure EV)
Too expensiveNOT BUYAt what price would you consider the pure EV too expensive and never buy it?
ExpensiveBUYAt what price does the pure EV feel expensive, but you would still consider buying it?
CheapBUYAt what price does the pure EV seem like a great deal or bargain — so reasonable you want to buy it right away?
Too cheapNOT BUYAt what price does the pure EV seem too cheap, making you doubt its quality so much you would not buy it?

Logical order for every valid respondent: too cheap < cheap < expensive < too expensive.

6. The four intersection points

PointIntersection ofMeaningEgg cake
PMC — Point of Marginal CheapnessExpensive (Graph 2, BUY) ∩ Too cheap (Graph 4, NOT BUY)Lower boundary of the acceptable price range. Below it, more people think it is “too cheap to buy” than “expensive” — they view the product negatively and with suspicion. At PMC the fear of low quality fades and people start to see it as a real price.≈ 68 Baht
PME — Point of Marginal ExpensivenessToo expensive (Graph 1, NOT BUY) ∩ Cheap (Graph 3, BUY)Upper boundary. Above it (e.g. at 100), most people say it’s too expensive and few think it’s a good value; the too-expensive line rises quickly and demand falls immediately.≈ 92 Baht

OPP — Optimal Price PointToo expensive (Graph 1) ∩ Too cheap (Graph 4) — the two NOT BUY curvesThe price that minimizes consumer resistance: it prevents too much rejection from either side, so the widest possible audience is reached.Between 70 and 80 Baht
IDP — Indifference Price PointExpensive (Graph 2) ∩ Cheap (Graph 3) — the two BUY curvesThe average price most people think is normal for the market: as many think it is expensive as think it is cheap. Memory hook

OPP = both NOT-BUY curves (too cheap × too expensive). IDP = both BUY curves (cheap × expensive). PMC and PME mix one of each: PMC = eXpensive × too cheap (low boundary); PME = too eXpensive × cheap (upper boundary). The “marginal cheapness” point uses the too cheap curve; the “marginal expensiveness” point uses the too expensive curve.

7. Worked example: “Healthy High-Protein Egg Cake”

A team of undergraduate entrepreneurship students created a “Healthy High-Protein Egg Cake” for students at Kasetsart University. Before launching, they ran an online Van Westendorp PSM survey that reached 100 people who often eat healthy foods or go to the gym. If a respondent’s answers did not follow the order too cheap < cheap < expensive < too expensive, the data were discarded. Data file: High Protein Egg.xlsx (columns id, too_cheap, cheap, expensive, too_expensive, intent_cheap, intent_expen). Task: find PMC, PME, OPP and IDP.

Stata steps

  1. Save the Excel file to a folder; open Stata; File → Import → Excel; select “High Protein Egg.xlsx”.
  2. Tick “Import first row as variable names” (the variable names are in the first row).
  3. Check the data with Data Editor (Browse) — you can view the data but cannot edit it.
  4. Open the Do-file Editor and the do-file “PSM_4lines.do”; highlight the whole code and click Run (Execute).
* 1. cumulative share (0–1) of each answer
cumul too_cheap,     gen(c_too_cheap) equal
cumul cheap,         gen(c_cheap) equal
cumul expensive,     gen(c_expensive) equal
cumul too_expensive, gen(c_too_expensive) equal
* 2. reverse the two "cheap" curves (scale 100, falling 100% → 0%)
gen pct_too_cheap     = (1 - c_too_cheap) * 100
gen pct_cheap         = (1 - c_cheap) * 100
* 3. the two "expensive" curves rise 0% → 100%
gen pct_expensive     = c_expensive * 100
gen pct_too_expensive = c_too_expensive * 100
* 4. draw the 4 lines
twoway (line pct_too_cheap too_cheap, sort lcolor(blue)) ///
       (line pct_cheap cheap, sort ...) ...

Notice how the code itself explains Section 5: cumul makes cumulative curves; 1 − cumulative makes the inverse curves for cheap and too cheap.

Results read from the graph: PMC ≈ 68, PME ≈ 92, IDP ≈ 83. The too-cheap line (solid blue) and the too-expensive line (dashed red) are clearly separate and never really cross; technically they only meet around 0% when the price is about 70–80 baht. Therefore OPP is between 70 and 80 Baht. Launch range: 68–92 baht.

8. Newton–Miller–Smith (NMS): forecasting an individual demand curve

The NMS model adds two more questions to the original four PSM questions to measure the intent-to-purchase scale:

  1. When the price is the one you called “cheap,” how likely are you to buy? (1–5 scale or 0–100%) → variable intent_cheap
  2. When the price is the one you called “expensive,” how likely are you to buy? (1–5 or 0–100%) → variable intent_expen

Steps for forecasting an individual demand curve: (1) find the appropriate prices on the demand curve; (2) convert the response scale into probability weights; (3) calculate the average probability of purchase for each respondent; (4) plot the demand curve at each price.

9. NMS step by step on the egg-cake data

Step 1 · Price grid

  • Find the minimum price in the data (lowest too_cheap): 42 baht → the program rounds it down to a round number: \(P_{min}=40\).
  • Find the maximum (highest too_expensive): 158 baht → rounds up to \(P_{max}=160\).
  • With 12 price points: \(\text{Interval}=\dfrac{P_{max}-P_{min}}{12}=\dfrac{160-40}{12}=\dfrac{120}{12}=10\).
  • Prices on the demand curve: 40, 50, 60, …, 160.

10. Revenue index and the market forecast

\[ \text{Expected revenue index (per one customer)}=\text{Price}\times\text{Probability of purchase at that price} \]
PriceProbability of purchaseExpected revenue index (per customer)
8030%80 × 30% = 24
8525%85 × 25% = 21.25
9020%90 × 20% = 18

(Teaching illustration. In the Stata output, “demand” is in percent units, so revenue = price × percent number, e.g. 80 × 29.65 = 2,372 — the same idea scaled by 100.)

Decision: the revenue curve peaks at 70 baht → “The entrepreneur should price the product at 70 Baht/unit to maximize the expected revenue per customer.” Note: the revenue-maximizing price (70) is inside the PSM acceptable range (68–92) and at the low end of the OPP band (70–80).

From one customer to the whole market

Assume the target population of high-protein-egg students at Kasetsart University is 72,000.

  • Market demand curve = 72,000 × probability of purchase at each price.
  • Total expected revenue = 72,000 × expected revenue index at each price.
\[ \text{At 70 Baht: total estimated revenue} \approx 2{,}450\times 72{,}000 = 176{,}400{,}000\ \text{Baht} \]
No-calculator multiplication

2,450 × 72 = 2,450 × 70 + 2,450 × 2 = 171,500 + 4,900 = 176,400; then × 1,000 → 176,400,000. (The slide rounds the 70-baht revenue reading 2,457 down to about 2,450.)

The Stata code for the individual demand and expected revenue index is in the do-file named “NMS demand curve and revenue”.

Recap — the 12 things to know cold

  1. WTP = reservation price = maximum price = height of the demand curve (1st unit 20, 10th unit 9).
  2. Two methods: CVM (hypothetical scenario) and Van Westendorp PSM (four prices).
  3. Double-bounded CVM: P0 = 60 first; yes → 80; no → 40. Based on competitor prices and costs.
  4. [yes,yes] ≥ 80 premium/loyal, skimming · [yes,no] 60–80 value-for-money, keep base price · [no,yes] 40–60 price-sensitive, discounts/economy version · [no,no] < 40 non-consumers, exclude.
  5. Cumulative shares 25% (80), 50% (60), 80% (40) → with 10,000 people: 2,500 / 5,000 / 8,000.
  6. PSM: too expensive (not buy), expensive (buy), cheap (buy), too cheap (not buy). Order too cheap < cheap < expensive < too expensive.
  7. Cumulative (rising): too expensive, expensive. Inverse cumulative (falling): cheap, too cheap.
  8. PMC = expensive ∩ too cheap ≈ 68 (lower bound). PME = too expensive ∩ cheap ≈ 92 (upper bound). Range 68–92.
  9. OPP = too cheap ∩ too expensive, minimizes resistance (70–80). IDP = cheap ∩ expensive, normal/current market price (≈ 83).
  10. NMS: +2 questions (likelihood at cheap and at expensive). Grid 40…160 step 10. Weights 5 → 0.7, 4 → 0.3, 1–3 → 0.
  11. Inside cheap–expensive → average the two weights; outside → 0. Averages: 29.65% (80), 17.85% (90), 9.75% (100). Too low a price (40–70) → quality doubts.
  12. Revenue index = price × probability; max at 70 baht (70 × 35.1 = 2,457). Market: 72,000 people; 2,450 × 72,000 = 176,400,000 baht.

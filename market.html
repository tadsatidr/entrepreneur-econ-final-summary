Chapter 5. Market demand analysis in practice

Willingness to pay is the demand curve

The slides also call willingness to pay the reservation price. It is the height of the demand curve at a given unit.

  • When the unit in question is the 1st unit, the consumer will pay up to 20 baht.
  • When the unit is the 10th, the consumer will pay up to 9 baht.

Two measurement methods are named: the contingent valuation method (CVM), and Van Westendorp’s price sensitivity meter (PSM).

Contingent valuation, double-bounded

Respondents state how much they would pay in a hypothetical scenario written into the questionnaire. The worked design is double-bounded: three prices chosen from competitor prices and costs, and each person answers two questions in sequence.

  • Original or base price, \(P_0 = 60\) baht. This is the initial bid.
  • Low price, \(P_L = 40\) baht.
  • High price, \(P_H = 80\) baht.

Question 1: would you buy the new product at 60 baht?

  • If yes, question 2 offers the higher bid. The script mentions premium materials and special features and asks about 80 baht.
  • If no, question 2 offers the lower bid. The script says the team could reduce costs and asks about 40 baht.
AnswersWillingness to paySegmentStrategy on the slide
[yes, yes] WTP \(\ge P_H\) (at least 80) Premium / loyal Primary target. Focus on them early. Offer the full-featured premium or luxury version and use price skimming to raise profit per unit.
[yes, no] \(P_0 \le\) WTP \( < P_H\) (60 up to, but not including, 80) Value-for-money Emphasize value and keep the base price.
[no, yes] \(P_L \le\) WTP \( < P_0\) (40 up to, but not including, 60) Price-sensitive / promotion Short-term sales from occasional discounts, discount codes, or a lower-spec economy version, which also collects data on this group.
[no, no] WTP \( < P_L\) (below 40) Non-consumers Exclude this group from all marketing communications.

The order of the pair is the order of the questions the person actually received. [yes, no] means yes to 60 and no to 80. [no, yes] means no to 60 and yes to 40.

Van Westendorp: four price perceptions

PSM does not ask for a single willingness-to-pay number. Each respondent gives four monetary amounts. The pure-EV wording on the slides:

QuestionBuy?What is being asked
Too expensiveNot buyThe price at which the EV is so expensive the person would never buy it.
ExpensiveBuyIt feels expensive, but the person would still consider buying.
CheapBuyA bargain: so reasonable that the person wants to buy right away.
Too cheapNot buySo cheap that quality is in doubt and the person would not buy.

Four graphs are built from those answers. The shape is part of the definition.

  1. Too expensive, not buy. Cumulative frequency. At a low starting price almost nobody calls it too expensive. As price rises, more people do. The curve rises.
  2. Expensive, buy. Cumulative frequency. As price rises, more people say it is expensive but they would still buy. The curve rises.
  3. Cheap, buy. Inverse cumulative frequency. At low prices many people call it cheap. That share falls as price rises.
  4. Too cheap, not buy. Inverse cumulative frequency. At very low prices many people suspect low quality and refuse to buy. As price rises, fewer people refuse for that reason. The curve falls.

The four intersections

PointWhich curves crossRoleFigure used on the slides
Point of marginal cheapness, PMC Expensive and too cheap Lower boundary of the acceptable range About 68 baht
Point of marginal expensiveness, PME Too expensive and cheap Upper boundary About 92 baht
Optimal price point, OPP The two not-buy curves, too cheap and too expensive The price that minimizes consumer resistance, so the widest audience is not pushed away on either side In the egg example, between 70 and 80 baht
Indifference price point, IDP Cheap and expensive, the two buy curves The price most people treat as normal. About as many call it expensive as call it cheap. Used as a reading of the current market price, or when the firm wants to look like good value About 83 baht on the egg graph

Acceptable launch prices are the range (PMC, PME). The slide’s sentence for the running diagram: a new product can be priced between 68 and 92 baht.

  • Below the PMC, for example at 63 baht, more people say “too cheap to buy” than “expensive but still buy.” The product is still viewed with suspicion.
  • At the PMC, the fear that a cheap product is low quality fades, and people start to see a real price.
  • Above the PME, for example at 100 baht, most people say it is too expensive and few still see a good value. The too-expensive curve rises quickly and demand falls.

The high-protein egg cake

Undergraduate entrepreneurship students at Kasetsart designed a “Healthy High-Protein Egg Cake” for KU students. Before launch they ran a Van Westendorp survey online. It reached 100 people who often eat healthy food or go to the gym. A response is discarded if the four prices are not in the order too cheap < cheap < expensive < too expensive. The file is High Protein Egg.xlsx. The task is to find PMC, PME, OPP, and IDP.

Stata path on the slides: save the Excel file, import it with variable names in the first row, open the do-file PSM_4lines.do, highlight the code, and run it. The resulting graph is read as PMC ≈ 68, PME ≈ 92, and IDP ≈ 83. The too-cheap curve (solid blue) and the too-expensive curve (dashed red) stay apart and do not cross in the ordinary way. They only meet around 0% near 70–80 baht, so the slide places OPP between 70 and 80 baht. One label on that graph also marks about 70 and about 80 around the OPP region.

Newton–Miller–Smith: from four prices to a demand curve

The NMS extension adds two intent-to-purchase questions to the original four prices:

  1. When the price is the one this person called cheap, how likely are they to buy? Answer on a 1–5 scale or a 0–100% scale.
  2. When the price is the one they called expensive, how likely are they to buy? Same scales.

Four steps:

  1. Choose the prices that will be plotted.
  2. Turn the response scale into probability weights.
  3. At each plotted price, compute each respondent’s purchase probability, then average across respondents.
  4. Plot the demand curve. The do-file named on the last slide is “NMS demand curve and revenue.”

Step 1. The price grid

Find the minimum stated price and the maximum. In the egg data the lowest price shown is 42 baht, and the program rounds it down to a round number, 40. The largest is 158 baht, rounded up to 160. If the curve uses 12 price points,

\[ \text{Interval} = \frac{P_{\max}-P_{\min}}{12} = \frac{160-40}{12} = \frac{120}{12} = 10 \]

The prices on the demand curve are 40, 50, 60, …, 160.

Step 2. Likert weights

When the likelihood question is a 5-point scale:

  • Score 5, definitely buy → 0.70.
  • Score 4, probably buy → 0.30.
  • Scores 1, 2, and 3, neutral through not buy → 0.

Step 3. One person’s weight at a test price

The worked rule at a test price of 80 baht: if that price lies between the person’s cheap price and expensive price, average the two weights. If it does not, the purchase weight is 0.

  • At 90 baht, 17.85%.

The plotted individual demand curve is not a straight downward line at the far left. The slide says that if the high-protein eggs are priced too low, about 40 to 70 baht a pack, buyers doubt quality: hormones, counterfeits, or stock near expiry. Those doubts reduce the probability of purchase. A separate caption on the plot says that, in that too-low region, a lower price goes with a lower purchase probability.

Expected revenue, then the whole market

The teaching definition:

\[ \text{Expected revenue index per customer} = \text{Price} \times \text{Probability of purchase at that price} \]
PriceProbabilityIndex per customer
8030%80 × 30% = 24
8525%85 × 25% = 21.25
9020%90 × 20% = 18

That small table is an illustration of the multiplication. The estimated egg-cake chart is a separate picture. Its demand panel is the probability-weighted purchase share. Its revenue panel is the expected revenue curve. The slide reads the revenue curve and says the entrepreneur should price the product at 70 baht per unit to maximize expected revenue per customer.

The target population of students for this product at Kasetsart is 72,000. Market demand at each price is that population times the purchase probability. Total expected revenue is the population times the revenue index. The slide’s arithmetic at the revenue-maximizing price:

\[ 2{,}450 \times 72{,}000 = 176{,}400{,}000 \text{ baht} \]

So 2,450 is the revenue-index reading they take off the chart at 70 baht, and 176.4 million baht is total expected revenue for the 72,000-person market. A fast multiplication: \(2{,}450 \times 72 = 2{,}450 \times 70 + 2{,}450 \times 2 = 171{,}500 + 4{,}900 = 176{,}400\), then three more zeros because 72,000 is 72 × 1,000.

No-calculator tips for this block

CVM is a path, not four unrelated labels. Yes at 60 goes up to 80. No at 60 goes down to 40. Write the pair in that order before you pick the segment.
Stack the buyers. At 80, only [yes, yes]. At 60, add [yes, no]. At 40, add [no, yes]. Shares 25, 50, 80. Of 10,000: 2,500, 5,000, 8,000.
Two curves rise, two fall. Cumulative curves (too expensive, expensive) rise with price. Inverse curves (cheap, too cheap) fall with price.
Pair the crossings. PMC = expensive ∩ too cheap = lower bound ≈ 68. PME = too expensive ∩ cheap = upper bound ≈ 92. IDP = cheap ∩ expensive ≈ 83. OPP = the resistance minimum, 70 to 80 in the egg graph. Launch inside (68, 92).
NMS weights are 70, 30, and 0. Average of 30 and 0 is 15. Average of 70 and 30 is 50. Outside the cheap-to-expensive interval, the weight is 0. Grid step: (160 − 40) / 12 = 10.
Revenue is a product you can do in your head. 80 × 0.3 = 24. The market illustration is 2,450 × 72,000 = 176,400,000, at the price the chart picks, 70 baht.

Chapters 3–4. Consumer behavior and the Bangkok EV study

Chapter 3 starts from the demand equation, then switches to the theory of planned behavior when intention and purchase come apart. Chapter 4 estimates that theory on a 550-person pure-EV survey and turns the tables into a 4P strategy.

Demand, restated

Demand is the willingness and ability of buyers to purchase different quantities of a good or service at different prices during a specific time period.

\[ Q_x^d = \beta_0 + \beta_1 P_x + \beta_2 P_z + \beta_3 Y + u \]

\(Q_x^d\) is quantity demanded of X. \(P_x\) is its price. \(P_z\) is the price of a related good, a substitute or a complement. \(Y\) is the consumer’s income. \(u\) stands for unobserved variables that affect \(Q_x^d\). The slides draw \(P_x\), \(P_z\), and \(Y\) as the observed variables, and \(u\) as the unobserved ones. \(Q_x^d\) itself is labeled willingness and ability to purchase.

When purchase happens, and when it does not

\(Q_{x1}\): purchasing occurred. Purchase intention equals the actual purchase. The slides call this the economics explanation, demand theory.

\(Q_{x2}\): purchasing did not occur. Intention only. Purchase intention is not the actual purchase. The slides call this the psychology explanation and introduce the theory of planned behavior.

TPB is what the deck uses once intention and behavior are allowed to differ. The diagram still has \(P_x\), \(P_z\), and \(Y\) in the picture, but the explanation that gets estimated in Chapter 4 is the psychological one.

The five TPB pieces, with the EV items

The running example is purchasing a pure electric vehicle. Chapter 3 gives the definitions and a short item list. Chapter 4’s questionnaire is the longer instrument that is actually averaged and regressed. Counts below are the Chapter 4 counts.

Attitude toward the behavior

Attitudes reflect the degree to which people evaluate a specific behavior as favorable or unfavorable.

  • ATT1. It makes my family happy to purchase a pure electric vehicle.
  • ATT2. It is wise to purchase a pure electric vehicle.
  • ATT3. Purchasing a pure electric vehicle makes my family fashion.
  • ATT4. Purchasing a pure electric vehicle is attractive to my family.
  • ATT5, on the Chapter 4 instrument. Purchasing a pure electric vehicle is beneficial to my family.

Subjective norm

Subjective norms refer to perceived social pressure on behavior accomplishment.

  • SN1. My family encourages me to purchase a pure electric vehicle.
  • SN2. Friends surrounding me encourage me.
  • SN3. Chapter 3 says “someone I respect supports buying a pure EV.” One blank of the Chapter 4 form says “my boss encourages me.” The filled coding example returns to “someone I respect.”
  • SN4. Experts or influencers I follow encourage me.
  • SN5, Chapter 4. People in my neighborhood encourage me.

Perceived behavioral control

Perceived behavioral control reflects people’s difficulty in implementation, and it reflects past experience and expected obstacles.

  • PBC1. Sufficient financial resources and situational readiness to purchase without hesitation.
  • PBC2. Full autonomy to make the final decision.
  • PBC3. Comfortable and stress-free when making the final decision.
  • PBC4, Chapter 4. Can decide regardless of public opinions or online drama.
  • PBC5. Enough clear information and technical knowledge. This is the item profiled later, and the strategy slide also treats it as technical confidence.
  • PBC6, Chapter 4. The necessary budget, cash flow, or financing.
  • PBC7, Chapter 4. A second technical-knowledge item.

Intention

Individual behavioral intention reflects how motivated individuals are to perform a specific behavior, that is, how much effort they will put into doing it.

  • INT1. When I buy a next car, I will consider buying a pure EV. This is the target outcome in the segmentation example.
  • INT2. I intend to choose a pure EV as my next car.
  • INT3. I will encourage friends and family to buy one.
  • INT4, Chapter 4. Willing to gather information and test-drive before the next purchase.
  • INT5, Chapter 4. Expect to purchase a pure EV within the next 3 to 5 years.

Behavior

Behavior covers observable actions, from preparatory steps such as test-driving and checking loans to the final action of making a purchase. The slide contrasts a cognitive, mental-state measure (intention) with two kinds of action: preparatory action, and the ultimate purchase.

  • BEH1. Visited EV showrooms or exhibitions to inspect pure EVs.
  • BEH2. Taken an actual test drive.
  • BEH3. Compared auto-loan rates and insurance for a pure EV.
  • BEH4. Started a formal step, such as a booking deposit or finance documents.
  • BEH5. Prepared the home: electricity meter or a wall-charger installation.

Answers use a 5-point Likert scale: 5 strongly agree, 4 agree, 3 neutral or undecided, 2 disagree, 1 strongly disagree.

Signs and the two equations

According to Ajzen (1991), as stated on the slides, a more positive attitude, a stronger positive subjective norm, and greater perceived behavioral control all enhance behavioral intention. Stronger positive intentions lead to a higher likelihood of performing the behavior. The resources and opportunities reflected by perceived behavioral control are also hypothesized to raise actual behavior.

\[ \text{intent} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{att} + \beta_2 \text{sn} + \beta_3 \text{pbc} + \varepsilon_1 \tag{1} \] \[ \text{beh} = \delta_0 + \delta_1 \text{intent} + \delta_2 \text{pbc} + \varepsilon_2 \tag{2} \]

Every slope in that diagram is marked positive: \(\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3, \delta_1, \delta_2 > 0\).

The composites are row means of the Likert items:

\[ \text{att}=\frac{\text{att}_1+\cdots+\text{att}_5}{5},\quad \text{sn}=\frac{\text{sn}_1+\cdots+\text{sn}_5}{5},\quad \text{pbc}=\frac{\text{pbc}_1+\cdots+\text{pbc}_7}{7} \] \[ \text{intent}=\frac{\text{int}_1+\cdots+\text{int}_5}{5},\quad \text{beh}=\frac{\text{beh}_1+\cdots+\text{beh}_5}{5} \]

What was estimated

Questionnaires were distributed in Bangkok by convenience sampling. 550 people responded. The file is EV.xlsx. The slides walk through importing that file, labeling variables, and attaching value labels. A label such as a yes/no label or an agreement label can be applied to more than one variable. The gender value label is 1 = male, 2 = female, 3 = LGBTQ+.

Stata commands on the estimation slides:

  • egen att = rowmean(att1-att5)
  • egen sn = rowmean(sn1-sn5)
  • egen pbc = rowmean(pbc1-pbc7)
  • egen intent = rowmean(int1-int5)
  • egen beh = rowmean(beh1-beh5)
  • reg intent att sn pbc
  • reg beh intent pbc

The name intent is deliberate. The slide notes that in Stata, int is a reserved keyword for the integer data type, so the row mean cannot be stored under that name.

The questionnaire also records demographics and the current car: gender, age band, marital status, education, family members, occupation, work experience, monthly income band, years with the current car, up to eight loan sources, nine reasons for using a conventional vehicle, the current car-loan band (car_debt), and other debt (other_debt). The segmentation example uses gender, int1, car_debt, and pbc5. You do not need every income bracket memorized; you need to know which variable the profile actually tabulates.

One filled questionnaire is shown as a coding demonstration (female, age 25 or under, single, bachelor’s degree, five family members, and so on). Those ticks are an illustration of data entry, not a finding about the 550.

Four steps, then the 4Ps

  1. Market segmentation. Group consumers with similar needs, behaviors, or characteristics. The example groups with gender and crosses it with the outcome.
  2. Segment naming. Give the group a name and a short description that a business can act on.
  3. Segment profiling. Use if conditions to scan financial constraints, drivers, and barriers, and write a persona.
  4. Data-driven strategy. Design the 4Ps from that profile. The slide’s line is to let the data drive the strategy rather than guess.

The worked assignment: an EV marketing consultant launching a pure EV in Bangkok, with the goal of raising int1 (intention to consider an EV for the next car). The assigned segment variable in the example is gender.

The worked segment: Ready-to-Buy Males

Command: tabulate gender int1, row. Row percentages, so each gender sums to 100%.

  • 132 male respondents, 40.87% of all male respondents, said they definitely want an electric vehicle for their next car (int1 = 5).
  • 70 female respondents, 33.18%, gave the same answer.
  • The slide’s conclusion: men are much more likely to be ready to buy, so the analysis focuses on males with a purchase-intention score of 5.

Segment name: “Ready-to-Buy Males.” Who they are: Bangkok-based male consumers in an urban market, with the highest readiness to switch to pure EVs. Mindset on the slide: they do not need further convincing about the benefits. They are at the final stage and are prepared to buy immediately. The Stata filter used from here on is gender == 1 & int1 == 5, and N = 132.

Profile 1. car_debt

Command: tabulate car_debt if gender == 1 & int1 == 5. The question is whether a remaining auto loan blocks an immediate switch.

  • 88.64% of this target group currently carry existing car debt.
  • 52.27% of them owe more than 1,500,000 baht on the current vehicle.

The slide’s interpretation: a score of 5 on int1 does not equal the financial ability to purchase. They want the EV now and are trapped by the current loan. Traditional advertising or a small cash discount will fail.

Profile 2. pbc5

Command: tabulate pbc5 if gender == 1 & int1 == 5. The stated objective is perceived difficulty with home charging and public charging. The finding the slide actually reports is about technical knowledge:

  • 90.15% of ready-to-buy males feel confident in their technical knowledge to purchase an EV.
  • Nearly all of them see no major barrier in the technology. The slide calls them tech-savvy, well informed, and confident with specifications and operation.

The package that follows those two findings

Strategic goal: remove the debt barrier and use the technical readiness to sell a premium EV in one transaction. The name on the slide is the “Financial Transition & Tech-Forward” EV package.

  1. Integrated financial engineering, from the car-debt table. Aggressive trade-in, buying back the current car at a premium so the remaining loan is offset. Debt consolidation that rolls the old loan into the new EV loan at a low rate. Balloon financing or flexible leasing with low payments in the early years.
  2. Ecosystem, from the pbc5 table. A complimentary high-performance home wall charger, because technical readiness is already 90.15%. The charger is described with smart scheduling, a mobile app, and bi-directional charging. It is framed as a reward for people who are already confident, not as a lesson for people who are not.

The slide’s three bullets:

  • All three latent variables, attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control, influence the intention to purchase a pure EV.
  • Among them, attitude is the most important factor for increasing that intention. The coefficients sit on the same 1-to-5 row-mean scale, and 0.317 is larger than 0.269 and 0.254. Every p-value is 0.000, so “most important” here is about size, not about which one is significant.
  • Attitude, social norms, and perceived behavioral control indirectly affect the decision to buy a pure EV.

R-squared 0.5058 says the three composites account for about half of the sample variation in intention. Prob > F = 0.0000 says the equation as a whole is significant. Each t statistic is above 6, so each slope is far below the 0.01 cutoff and would take ***.

The slide’s two bullets:

  • Intention is the only factor that influences the purchase of a pure EV.
  • Perceived behavioral control has no effect on this purchase behavior.

That second bullet is the direct coefficient in equation (2). Its p-value is 0.177, which is above 0.10, so you fail to reject \(H_0: \delta_2 = 0\) even at 10%. The point estimate is negative, about −0.099, but the slide does not interpret the sign; it says there is no effect. The hypothesis on the diagram was positive. The data do not support that direct positive effect.

The indirect path is the other bullet from the first regression. Attitude, subjective norm, and PBC all raise intention, and intention raises behavior (coefficient about 0.839, t = 11.48). R-squared on the behavior equation is lower, 0.2452. The model F test is still significant. The intercept’s p-value is 0.034, so the constant itself is significant at 5%, which is not one of the slide’s conclusion bullets.

Hold both results at once. PBC matters for intention (p = 0.000 in equation 1). PBC does not have a direct effect on behavior (p = 0.177 in equation 2). It can still matter for behavior through intention. That is the “indirectly” sentence.

No-calculator tips for this block

Sort the item before you sort the coefficient. Happy, wise, fashion, attractive, beneficial: attitude. Family, friends, boss, experts, neighbors: subjective norm. Money, autonomy, stress, knowledge: PBC. “I will / I intend / I expect”: intention. “I have visited / driven / compared / paid / installed”: behavior.
Equation (1) has three slopes. Equation (2) has two. PBC is in both. Attitude and subjective norm are only in the intention equation. Behavior is never a regressor in these slides.
Compare p with 0.10, not with the coefficient. 0.000 is significant. 0.177 is not. A negative −0.099 does not become a finding when the p-value fails.
“Most important” is the largest intention slope. 0.317 > 0.269 > 0.254. All three are significant, so the ranking is by size. The 0.839 belongs to intention in the behavior equation; do not mix the two tables.
The four percentages. 40.87% of men (132 people) and 33.18% of women (70 people) are at int1 = 5. Of the 132 men, 88.64% have car debt, 52.27% owe more than 1.5 million baht, and 90.15% are technically confident.
R-squared is not a star. 0.5058 and 0.2452 describe fit. Significance of one variable is the p-value column. Both equations have Prob > F = 0.0000.

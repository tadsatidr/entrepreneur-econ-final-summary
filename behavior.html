Chapter 3 · Consumer behavior dynamics

1. Demand restated: observed and unobserved variables

Demand is the willingness and ability of buyers to purchase different quantities of a good or service at different prices during a specific time period.

\[ Q_x^d=\beta_0+\beta_1P_x+\beta_2P_z+\beta_3Y+u \]

\(Q_x^d\) quantity demanded of X; \(P_x\) price of X; \(P_z\) price of a related good Z (substitute or complement); \(Y\) consumer income; \(u\) = unobserved variables affecting \(Q_x^d\). The slides split the right-hand side into:

  • Observed variables: \(P_x, P_z, Y\) — we can collect them in a table (person 1…10, old and new prices, income, quantity).
  • Unobserved variables: \(u\) — things like feelings, social pressure and confidence that demand theory does not measure.

\(Q_x^d\) itself is labelled “willingness and ability to purchase”.

2. When intention and purchase are not the same

\(Q_{x1}\): Purchasing occurred

Purchase intention = actual purchase. The person wanted it and bought it. This is the economics explanation (demand theory): price, related prices and income explain quantity.

\(Q_{x2}\): Purchasing not occurred / intention only

Purchase intention ≠ actual purchase. The person intends to buy but hasn’t. Demand theory cannot explain the gap, so the slides use the psychology explanation: the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Both purchase intention and actual purchase are driven by unobserved variables.

What it meansEconomics explains what people buy; psychology (TPB) explains what they intend and why intention does or doesn’t become action.
Why it mattersA firm with many “interested” customers but few sales must find the barrier between intention and behavior.
On the examMatch: “purchase occurred” → demand theory; “intention only” → TPB.

3. The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)

Three psychological factors → Intention → Behavior, with Perceived Behavioral Control also pointing directly at Behavior.

4. The five constructs, defined and measured

Every item is answered on a 5-point Likert scale: 5 = Strongly Agree, 4 = Agree, 3 = Neutral/Undecided, 2 = Disagree, 1 = Strongly Disagree. Chapter 3 lists short item sets; the Chapter 4 questionnaire (which is actually analysed) has the longer sets marked “Ch 4”.

① Attitude toward behavior (ATT) — 5 items

Definition: Attitudes reflect the degree to which people evaluate a specific behavior as favorable or unfavorable.

  1. att1 It makes my family happy to purchase a pure electric vehicle.
  2. att2 It is wise to purchase a pure electric vehicle.
  3. att3 Purchasing a pure electric vehicle makes my family fashion.
  4. att4 Purchasing a pure electric vehicle is attractive to my family.
  5. att5 (Ch 4) Purchasing a pure electric vehicle is beneficial to my family.

② Subjective norm (SN) — 5 items

Definition: Subjective norms refer to perceived social pressure on behavior accomplishment.

  1. sn1 My families encourage me to purchase a pure electric vehicle.
  2. sn2 Friends surrounding me encourage me to purchase a pure electric vehicle.
  3. sn3 Someone I respect supports buying a pure EV car. (The blank Ch 4 form words sn3 as “My boss encourages me…”; the filled example uses “Someone I respect”.)
  4. sn4 Experts or influencers I follow encourage me to purchase a pure electric vehicle.
  5. sn5 (Ch 4) People in my neighborhood encourage me to purchase a pure electric vehicle.

③ Perceived behavioral control (PBC) — 7 items

Definition: Perceived behavioral control reflects people’s difficulty in implementation and reflects past experiences and expected difficulties or obstacles.

  1. pbc1 I have sufficient financial resources and situational readiness to purchase an EV without hesitation.
  2. pbc2 I have full autonomy and independence to make the final decision to buy an EV by myself.
  3. pbc3 I feel comfortable and stress-free when making the final decision to purchase an EV.
  4. pbc4 (Ch 4) I can confidently make my own decision to buy an EV regardless of public opinions or online drama.
  5. pbc5 I possess enough clear information and technical knowledge to confidently purchase an EV. (Ch 3 numbers this PBC4.)
  6. pbc6 (Ch 4) I currently have the necessary budget, cash flow, or financing opportunities to purchase an EV.
  7. pbc7 (Ch 4) I feel I have enough clear information and technical knowledge to confidently buy an EV.

④ Intention (INT) — 5 items

Definition: Individual behavioral intention reflects how motivated individuals are to perform a specific behavior, that is, how much effort they will put into doing it.

  1. int1 When I buy a (next) car, I will consider buying a pure electric vehicle. ← the target outcome in the Ch 4 case
  2. int2 I intend to choose a pure electric vehicle as my next car. (Ch 4 form: “I will encourage my friends…”; order differs slightly between decks.)
  3. int3 I will encourage my friends and family to buy a pure electric vehicle.
  4. int4 (Ch 4) I am willing to make an effort to gather information and test-drive a pure EV before my next car purchase.
  5. int5 (Ch 4) I expect to purchase a pure electric vehicle as my next car within the next 3 to 5 years.

⑤ Behavior (BEH) — 5 items

Definition: Behavior covers a range of observable actions, from preparatory steps such as test-driving and checking loans to the final action of making a purchase.

INTENTIONBEHAVIOR
Measurement scopeCognitive readiness (mental state)1. Preparatory action: e.g. information search, test drive, loan checks
2. Ultimate action: e.g. final purchase & adoption
  1. beh1 I have visited EV showrooms or automotive exhibitions to physically inspect Pure EVs.
  2. beh2 I have taken actual test drives of Pure EVs.
  3. beh3 I have compared specific auto loan rates and insurance options for buying a Pure EV.
  4. beh4 I have initiated formal transactional steps, such as placing a booking deposit or submitting finance documents.
  5. beh5 I have prepared my residential infrastructure, such as upgrading the electricity meter or arranging for a Wall Charger installation.
Exam trap: intention vs behavior items

Intention items are about the future (“I will consider”, “I intend”, “I am willing”, “I expect within 3–5 years”). Behavior items are things already done (“I have visited / taken test drives / compared loans / placed a deposit / prepared my home”). “Willing to test-drive” = intention (int4); “have taken a test drive” = behavior (beh2).

5. The hypotheses and the two equations

Ajzen (1991), equation (1): a more positive attitude, a stronger positive subjective norm, and greater perceived behavioral control all enhance behavioral intention.

Ajzen (1991), equation (2): stronger positive intentions lead to a higher likelihood of behavior achievement. Also, the resources and opportunities a person has — reflected by perceived behavioral control — positively influence their actual behavior.

\[ \text{intent}=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\text{att}+\beta_2\,\text{sn}+\beta_3\,\text{pbc}+\varepsilon_1 \qquad (1) \] \[ \text{beh}=\delta_0+\delta_1\,\text{intent}+\delta_2\,\text{pbc}+\varepsilon_2 \qquad (2) \]

Hypothesized signs: \(\beta_1(+),\ \beta_2(+),\ \beta_3(+),\ \delta_1(+),\ \delta_2(+)\). Note that pbc appears in both equations (it can affect behavior directly and through intention); att and sn appear only in (1).

Self-check (Chapter 3)
  1. “My boss encourages me to buy an EV” measures…? → Subjective norm (social pressure).
  2. “It is wise to purchase a pure EV” measures…? → Attitude.
  3. “I have sufficient financial resources…” measures…? → Perceived behavioral control.
  4. Which construct is the dependent variable in equation (2)? → Behavior (beh).

7. Getting the data into Stata and labelling it

  1. Save the Excel file (EV.xlsx) to a folder on your computer.
  2. Open Stata → File → Import → Excel spreadsheet (*.xls; *.xlsx) → Browse → select EV.xlsx.
  3. Tick “Import first row as variable names” (row 1 of Excel holds the names gender, age, status…), then OK. Result: 550 observations.
  4. Label variables (describe what a variable is): e.g. label member as “Family Member”, loansource1 as “Cash/ Personal Saving”. You can change the label but not the name in the Properties window.
  5. Label values (describe what each code means): for gender, create a value label with 1 = male, 2 = female, 3 = LGBTQ+ (Manage value labels → Create label → Add). Repeat for other variables.
  6. A single value label such as YesNoLabel (1 Yes, 0 No) or AgreementLabel (1 Strongly Disagree, 2 Disagree, 3 Neutral/Undecided, 4 Agree, 5 Strongly Agree) can be applied to multiple variables.

8. Building the five scores (row means)

Each construct is the average of its items for that respondent (a “row mean” — one row = one person):

\[ \text{att}=\frac{att_1+\dots+att_5}{5},\quad \text{sn}=\frac{sn_1+\dots+sn_5}{5},\quad \text{pbc}=\frac{pbc_1+\dots+pbc_7}{7},\quad \text{intent}=\frac{int_1+\dots+int_5}{5},\quad \text{beh}=\frac{beh_1+\dots+beh_5}{5} \]
* STEP 1: Generate variables att, sn, pbc, int, beh
egen att    = rowmean(att1-att5)
egen sn     = rowmean(sn1-sn5)
egen pbc    = rowmean(pbc1-pbc7)
egen intent = rowmean(int1-int5)   /* In STATA, int is a reserved keyword used to specify the integer data type. */
egen beh    = rowmean(beh1-beh5)
Exam favourite

Why is the variable called intent and not int? Because int is a reserved keyword in Stata (the integer data type). And pbc is divided by 7, not 5.

No-calculator reading

To find the “most important” factor, compare coefficients within one regression (all on the same 1–5 scale): .3168 vs .2694 vs .2538 → attitude. To find the insignificant one, scan the P>|t| column for anything above 0.10 → pbc in eq. (2) at 0.177. Don’t confuse pbc’s two roles: significant for intention (0.254***), not significant for behavior (−0.099, p = 0.177).

10. The 4-step data-driven marketing strategy

  1. Market segmentation — the process of grouping consumers with similar needs, behaviors, or characteristics into distinct groups (e.g. grouping by gender or age in Stata).
  2. Segment naming — giving a meaningful name and a brief description to each segment based on their core traits, making it actionable for business decisions.
  3. Segment profiling — deeply analyzing the selected segment’s financial constraints, core drivers, and key barriers (e.g. using if conditions in Stata to scan debt, attitude, and obstacles) to create a clear customer persona.
  4. Data-driven strategy formulation — “Turning customer insights into actionable business decisions.” Once you have the customer profile, design a marketing strategy (4Ps) that directly responds to the data. Never guess — let the data drive your strategy.

Step 4 · Strategic marketing implications

Strategic goal: unlock the high-value segment by removing their debt barrier and leveraging their technical readiness to sell a premium EV in one smooth transaction.

The solution: the “Financial Transition & Tech-Forward” EV Package

1. Integrated financial engineering (driven by car_debt)

  • Aggressive trade-in programs: partner with used-car networks to buy back current cars at a premium, offsetting the remaining loan.
  • Debt consolidation: work with financial institutions to roll the remaining car debt into the new EV loan under a low-interest framework.
  • Balloon financing / flexible leasing: low monthly payments in the early years to accommodate tight cash flow.

2. Advanced ecosystem integration (driven by pbc5)

  • Complimentary high-performance home charging kit: because customers have high technical readiness (90.15%), reward them by bundling the EV with a premium smart home wall charger — smart scheduling, mobile-app control and bi-directional charging.
Exam trap

Which finding justifies the financing package? car_debt (88.64% indebted, 52.27% > 1.5 M). Which justifies the wall-charger bundle? pbc5 (90.15% technically confident). Why not a small cash discount? Because the barrier is debt, not price awareness or motivation.

Recap — the 12 things to know cold

  1. \(Q_x^d=\beta_0+\beta_1P_x+\beta_2P_z+\beta_3Y+u\); observed: Px, Pz, Y; unobserved: u.
  2. Qx1 purchase occurred (intention = purchase) → demand theory. Qx2 intention only → TPB (psychology).
  3. TPB: att, sn, pbc → intention → behavior; pbc → behavior too. Ajzen (1991). All hypothesized +.
  4. Attitude = favorable/unfavorable evaluation. SN = perceived social pressure. PBC = difficulty, past experience, expected obstacles. Intention = motivation/effort. Behavior = observable actions (preparatory → ultimate).
  5. 5-point Likert, 5 = strongly agree. Items: att 5, sn 5, pbc 7, int 5, beh 5.
  6. Bangkok, convenience sampling, 550 respondents, EV.xlsx; import with “first row as variable names”.
  7. egen x = rowmean(...); intent because int is a reserved keyword.
  8. Eq (1): att .317, sn .269, pbc .254, all p = 0.000 → all matter, attitude most important; R² 0.5058.
  9. Eq (2): intent .839 (p = 0.000) only factor; pbc −.099 (p = 0.177) no effect; R² 0.2452. att/sn/pbc work indirectly.
  10. 4 steps: segmentation → naming → profiling → strategy (4Ps). “Never guess — let the data drive.”
  11. tabulate gender int1, row: 132 males = 40.87% (females 33.18%) → “Ready-to-Buy Males”, N = 132.
  12. car_debt: 88.64% in debt, 52.27% > 1.5 M baht → trade-in, consolidation, balloon. pbc5: 90.15% confident → smart wall charger bundle.

Law of demand

When the good’s own price rises, quantity demanded falls. When that price falls, quantity demanded rises. The slides write this as \(P \uparrow \rightarrow Q^d \downarrow\) and \(P \downarrow \rightarrow Q^d \uparrow\).

A shift of demand is not a movement along it

The slide titled “Demand Rises” draws a rightward shift from \(D_1\) to \(D_2\) and labels it an increase in demand. At a price of 60 dollars, the blue-jeans quantity on that diagram goes from 600 on \(D_1\) (point A) to 900 on \(D_2\) (point B).

Four slides then give the reasons demand for blue jeans rises:

  • Income increases, so people buy more blue jeans.
  • Greater preference for the good.
  • A higher price of a substitute.
  • A lower price of a complement.

The next slide, “Quantity Demand Decreases,” is a different object. It shows a movement from A to B along a single curve \(D_1\). The caption says a change in quantity demanded is a movement along the demand curve, and the factor that causes it is the good’s own price.

The exam distinction. Own price changes quantity demanded (a slide along the curve). Income, preference, the substitute’s price, and the complement’s price change demand (a shift of the curve).

Own-price elasticity

The effect is the percent change in quantity. The cause is the percent change in price. Using the average (midpoint) price and quantity:

\[ E_{Q_x,P_x} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta P_x} = \frac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta P_x} \times \frac{\bar P_x}{\bar Q_x^d} \]

with \(\bar P_x = (P_1+P_2)/2\) and \(\bar Q_x^d = (Q_1+Q_2)/2\).

Interpretation when the elasticity is −2

The slide states \(E_{Q_x,P_x} = -2\) and reads it as: if the firm lowers its price by 1%, sales are expected to rise by 2%. The minus sign is the direction. The 2 is the size.

Worked example on the slides

Point A is \((Q,P) = (100, 10)\). Point B is \((50, 12)\).

  • \(\Delta P = 12 - 10 = 2\)
  • \(\Delta Q^d = 50 - 100 = -50\)
  • \(\bar P = (10+12)/2 = 11\)
  • \(\bar Q^d = (50+100)/2 = 75\)
  • \(\%\Delta P = (2/11)\times 100 = 18.2\%\)
  • \(\%\Delta Q^d = (-50/75)\times 100 = -66.7\%\)

The slide then writes

\[ E_{Q_x,P_x} = \frac{-66.7\%}{18.2\%} = 3.67 \]

It reports the size, 3.67, and the classification rule that follows uses that size. The two percent changes have opposite signs, which is the law of demand. Because 3.67 is greater than 1, this demand curve is elastic. A mental check without the decimal: 50/75 is two thirds, about 67%, and 2/11 is a bit under one fifth, about 18%. Quantity’s percent change is several times the price’s, so the curve is elastic even before you divide 66.7 by 18.2.

Price elasticity and total revenue

Total revenue is also called total expenditure on these slides. The deck fills in the same sentence twice, once for a 10% price increase and once for a 10% price decrease.

DemandQuantity response to a 10% price changePrice up: revenuePrice down: revenue
Elastic, \(E_d > 1\)Quantity changes by more than 10%FallsRises
Unit elastic, \(E_d = 1\)Quantity changes by exactly 10%UnchangedUnchanged
Inelastic, \(E_d < 1\)Quantity changes by less than 10%RisesFalls

Direction of quantity is still the law of demand: price up, quantity down; price down, quantity up. The revenue result depends on whether that quantity change is larger than, equal to, or smaller than the price change.

Cross-price and income elasticity

\[ E_{Q_x,P_z} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta P_z} = \frac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta P_z}\times\frac{\bar P_z}{\bar Q_x^d} \qquad E_{Q_x,Y} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta Y} = \frac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta Y}\times\frac{\bar Y}{\bar Q_x^d} \]

Cross-price, \(E_c\). Positive: the two goods are substitutes. Negative: they are complements.

Income, \(E_y\). Positive: X is normal. Inside that branch, \(E_y > 1\) is a luxury and \(E_y < 1\) is a necessity. Negative: X is inferior.

The necessity bullet is nested under the normal-good branch. An inferior good has negative income elasticity; it is not “a necessity” in this classification.

Chapter 2. The demand regression

The demand function uses quantity demanded of X, the price of X, the price of a related good Z (substitute or complement), and income Y.

\[ \text{Population: }\quad Q_x^d = \beta_0 + \beta_1 P_x + \beta_2 P_z + \beta_3 Y + u \] \[ \text{Sample: }\quad Q_x^d = b_0 + b_1 P_x + b_2 P_z + b_3 Y + e \]

\(\beta\) is the population parameter, the true value. \(b\) is the sample estimator, the estimated coefficient. The slides are explicit that we do not know \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3\). We estimate them and test.

For each slope the null is that the parameter is zero and the alternative is that it is not zero. The examples:

  • \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) against \(H_1: \beta_1 \neq 0\) (the own price).
  • \(H_0: \beta_2 = 0\) against \(H_1: \beta_2 \neq 0\) (the related price).
  • \(H_0: \beta_3 = 0\) against \(H_1: \beta_3 \neq 0\) (income).

Reject \(H_0\) when the p-value is below \(\alpha\). Alpha may be 0.01, 0.05, or 0.10.

How to read a coefficient once you have decided

  • Reject \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) (p-value \( < \alpha \)). A 1-baht increase in \(P_x\) leads to an average change in quantity of \(b_1\) units, holding the other factors constant.
  • Fail to reject (p-value \( > \alpha \)). A 1-baht increase in \(P_x\) has no statistically significant effect on quantity, holding the other factors constant.

The same sentence is used for \(P_z\) with \(b_2\), and for income with \(b_3\). The slide’s wording is “an average change of \(b\) units,” so the sign of \(b\) supplies the direction.

Elasticity from a linear regression

These slides estimate a linear demand equation. The coefficient is a slope in units, not a percent elasticity. A log-log regression, where the coefficient itself would be the elasticity, is not in this deck. Do not read \(b_1\) as “percent.”

If the null is rejected, convert the slope at the sample means:

\[ E_d = b_1 \times \frac{\bar P_x}{\bar Q_x^d} \qquad E_c = b_2 \times \frac{\bar P_z}{\bar Q_x^d} \qquad E_Y = b_3 \times \frac{\bar Y}{\bar Q_x^d} \]

If you fail to reject \(H_0: \beta = 0\), the slide sets that estimated elasticity to 0. The ratio of means is not used.

Mental check of −0.31. 37.8 / 5.5 is about 7, so 5.5/37.8 is about 1/7. Two-sevenths of 2.16 is about 0.3. Sign comes from the slope. Inelastic, because 0.3 is less than 1.

Elasticities at the means

Only the price coefficient is significant, so only the price elasticity is computed. Means: \(\bar Q = 175\), price 15.1, advertising 125, GPP 17.95.

\[ E_{\text{price}} = b_1 \times \frac{\overline{\text{Price}}}{\bar Q} = -12.202 \times \frac{15.1}{175} \]

Recompute this product. The slide prints the result of that multiplication as −1.503. The product \(-12.202 \times 15.1 / 175\) is about −1.05, not −1.503. On the exam, use the formula. A fast bound: \(12 \times 15 / 175 = 180/175\), a little over 1. So the size is a bit above 1 and this demand is slightly elastic. Advertising elasticity is written as 0. GPP elasticity is written as 0.

The same slide shows the simple-regression column of the elasticity table as “NA” for advertising and for GPP, because that model has no advertising or GPP regressor. Price elasticity in a simple regression is still \(b_1 \times \bar P / \bar Q\).

No-calculator tips for this block

Classify, then stop. Substitutes, complements, normal, inferior, luxury, and necessity are sign rules and a comparison with 1. They do not need a product.
Total revenue is a 3-by-2 table. Elastic moves revenue with the opposite of the price change. Inelastic moves revenue with the price change. Unit elastic leaves it alone.
Stars: compare with 0.10, then 0.05, then 0.01. 0.000 beats all three. 0.095 beats only 0.10. 0.57 beats none. 0.037 beats 0.10 and 0.05, so **.
Slope and elasticity are different sentences. “Falls by 2.1576 units” is the slope. “Falls by 0.31%” is the elasticity. Insignificant slope: say “unchanged,” and set elasticity to 0.
Means you should recognize. Simple example: quantity 37.8, price 5.5, elasticity −0.31. Multiple example: quantity 175, price 15.1, ads 125, GPP 17.95.
Percent versus units. A coefficient of −12.203 on quantity measured in thousands is about 12,200 units, which is the slide’s wording. It is not a 12.203% elasticity.

Chapter 1 · Individual demand and elasticity

1. Demand and the law of demand

Demand is the willingness and ability of buyers to purchase different quantities of a good or service at different prices during a specific time period. (Chapter 3 restates this definition with “willingness and ability” and “purchase” highlighted — both words matter: wanting something you cannot afford is not demand.)

The law of demand: when the good’s own price rises, quantity demanded falls; when the price falls, quantity demanded rises. The slide writes it as \(P\uparrow \rightarrow Q_d\downarrow\) and \(P\downarrow \rightarrow Q_d\uparrow\). That is why a demand curve slopes downward.

Why it mattersFirms sell to the market, not to one person. Chapter 5 builds market demand the same way (share of buyers × number of people).

At the lower price ($11) every buyer wants more, so the market curve also slopes down — the law of demand holds for the market too.

3. A shift of demand vs a movement along demand

This is the most common conceptual trap in Chapter 1. Two different things can happen to a demand curve:

Change in demand = the whole curve shifts

Caused by anything other than the good’s own price. The blue-jeans slides give four reasons demand rises (shifts right):

  1. Income increases → people buy more blue jeans.
  2. Greater preference for the good.
  3. Higher price of a substitute (a good used instead of jeans) → people switch to jeans.
  4. Lower price of a complement (a good used with jeans) → the package is cheaper, so more jeans are bought.

Reverse each one (income falls, preference falls, substitute gets cheaper, complement gets dearer) and demand shifts left.

Change in quantity demanded = a movement along the curve

Caused only by the good’s own price. The slide’s caption: “A change in quantity demanded is a movement along the demand curve. A change in this factor will cause a change in quantity demanded: (own) price.”

Price of jeans up → move up-left along D1 (fewer jeans). Price down → move down-right.

Exam trap

“The price of blue jeans rose, so demand for blue jeans decreased.” Wrong wording. Own price changes quantity demanded (movement), not demand (shift). And watch the complement direction: a lower complement price raises demand; a higher substitute price raises demand.

4. Price elasticity of demand

What it meansElasticity measures how strongly quantity reacts to price, in percent terms: “if price changes by 1%, by how many % does quantity change?”
Why it mattersIt tells a firm whether raising the price will raise or lower total revenue (section 7).
On the examCompute a percent change with midpoints, divide, then compare the size with 1.

The slide labels the top of the fraction the effect (% change in Q) and the bottom the cause (% change in P):

\[ E_{Q_x,P_x}=\frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta P_x} =\frac{\dfrac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\bar Q_x^d}\times 100}{\dfrac{\Delta P_x}{\bar P_x}\times 100} =\frac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta P_x}\times\frac{\bar P_x}{\bar Q_x^d} \]
SymbolMeaning
\(E_{Q_x,P_x}\)Own-price elasticity of demand for good X (also written \(E_d\)).
\(\Delta Q_x^d = Q_2-Q_1\)Change in quantity demanded (new minus old).
\(\Delta P_x = P_2-P_1\)Change in the good’s own price (new minus old).
\(\bar Q_x^d=(Q_1+Q_2)/2\)Average (midpoint) quantity — the base for the percent change.
\(\bar P_x=(P_1+P_2)/2\)Average (midpoint) price.
\(\times 100\)Turns a fraction into a percent; it cancels top and bottom, which is why the last form has no 100.

Reading a number. The slide shows \(E_{Q_x,P_x}=-2\) and says: “If the firm lowers its price by 1%, sales are expected to rise by 2%.” The minus sign gives the direction (opposite movement, the law of demand). The size (2) gives the strength. Equivalently, a 1% price rise cuts sales by 2%.

5. Worked example from the slides (every step)

On a straight demand curve, point A is price $10 with quantity 100, and point B is price $12 with quantity 50. The firm raises its price from A to B.

  1. Change in price: \(\Delta P = P_2-P_1 = 12-10 = 2\).
  2. Change in quantity: \(\Delta Q_d = Q_{d2}-Q_{d1} = 50-100 = -50\).
  3. Average price: \(\bar P = \dfrac{P_1+P_2}{2}=\dfrac{10+12}{2}=\dfrac{22}{2}=11\).
  4. Average quantity: \(\bar Q_d=\dfrac{Q_{d1}+Q_{d2}}{2}=\dfrac{50+100}{2}=\dfrac{150}{2}=75\).
  5. Percent change in price: \(\%\Delta P=\dfrac{2}{11}\times 100 = 18.2\%\) (2 ÷ 11 = 0.1818…).
  6. Percent change in quantity: \(\%\Delta Q_d=\dfrac{-50}{75}\times 100=-66.7\%\) (50 ÷ 75 = 2/3 = 0.667).
  7. Elasticity: \(E=\dfrac{-66.7\%}{18.2\%}\). The slide writes the result as 3.67 — the size. With its sign it is −3.67.

Shortcut route (same answer): \(\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P}\times\dfrac{\bar P}{\bar Q}=\dfrac{-50}{2}\times\dfrac{11}{75}=-25\times\dfrac{11}{75}=-\dfrac{275}{75}=-3.67\). Notice \(275/75 = 11/3 = 3\tfrac{2}{3}\) — easy by hand.

No-calculator check

−66.7 ÷ 18.2: 18.2 × 3 = 54.6 and 18.2 × 4 = 72.8, so the answer is between 3 and 4. Since 3.67 > 1, demand between A and B is elastic. For a classification question you can stop as soon as you see that the quantity % change (67%) is much bigger than the price % change (18%).

7. Elasticity and total revenue (total expenditure)

Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity. The slides also call it total expenditure (what buyers spend). When price changes, P and Q move in opposite directions, so TR follows whichever changes by the larger percentage.

8. Cross-price and income elasticity

Same idea, different “cause” in the denominator.

\[ E_{Q_x,P_z}=\frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta P_z}=\frac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta P_z}\times\frac{\bar P_z}{\bar Q_x^d} \qquad\qquad E_{Q_x,Y}=\frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta Y}=\frac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta Y}\times\frac{\bar Y}{\bar Q_x^d} \]

\(P_z\) is the price of another good Z; \(Y\) is consumer income. For these two, the sign is the whole story:

Cross-price elasticity \(E_c\)

  • \(E_c>0\) → X and Z are substitutes (Z dearer → buy more X).
  • \(E_c<0\) → X and Z are complements (Z dearer → buy less X).

Slide table (USDA): meat and dairy −0.21 (negative → complements by the rule); bread and cereal 0.02 and coffee and juice 0.07 (positive → substitutes, but very weak).

Income elasticity \(E_y\)

  • \(E_y>0\) → X is a normal good.
    • \(E_y>1\) → luxury (slide pictures: watch, sports car, jewellery).
    • \(E_y<1\) → necessity (everyday staples).
  • \(E_y<0\) → X is an inferior good (buy less as income rises).
Exam trap

“Necessity” and “luxury” are both normal goods (positive \(E_y\)). A necessity has \(0<E_y<1\); it is not the same as inferior (\(E_y<0\)). And cross-price elasticity of −0.21 means complements even though the number is small.

Self-check (Chapter 1) — try before opening
  1. Income rises and quantity of good X bought falls. Type of good? → Inferior (\(E_y<0\)).
  2. Price rises 10%, revenue rises. Elastic or inelastic? → Inelastic (quantity fell by less than 10%).
  3. The price of a complement falls. What happens to demand for X? → Shifts right (rises).
  4. Midpoint price between $10 and $12? → $11.

Chapter 2 · Estimating demand with regression

9. From a demand function to a regression model

The demand function says quantity depends on several things: \(Q_x^d=f(P_x, P_z, Y, \dots)\), where \(Q_x^d\) is quantity demanded of X, \(P_x\) its own price, \(P_z\) the price of a related good Z (substitute or complement) and \(Y\) the consumer’s income. To measure these effects we write a straight-line (linear) model:

\[ \text{Population regression model:}\quad Q_x^d=\beta_0+\beta_1P_x+\beta_2P_z+\beta_3Y+u \] \[ \text{Sample regression model:}\quad Q_x^d=b_0+b_1P_x+b_2P_z+b_3Y+e \quad\text{or}\quad \hat Q_x^d=b_0+b_1P_x+b_2P_z+b_3Y \]
Population parameter (true, unknown)Sample estimator (estimated coefficient)What it measures
\(\beta_1\)\(b_1=\dfrac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta P_x}\)Change in quantity when own price rises by 1 (other things constant)
\(\beta_2\)\(b_2=\dfrac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta P_z}\)Change in quantity when the related good’s price rises by 1
\(\beta_3\)\(b_3=\dfrac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta Y}\)Change in quantity when income rises by 1
\(\beta_0\)\(b_0\)Intercept (constant, Stata’s _cons)

\(u\) is the population error (everything else that affects demand); \(e\) is the sample residual; the hat in \(\hat Q\) means “fitted / predicted”. Key idea from the slide: “We don’t know the true value of parameters \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3\). We need to guess them” — we estimate them with data and then test them.

10. Hypothesis testing: is the effect real?

For each slope we test whether the true effect could be zero:

CoefficientNull hypothesis \(H_0\)Alternative \(H_1\)In words
\(\beta_1\)\(\beta_1=0\) (Px does not affect \(Q_x^d\))\(\beta_1\neq 0\) (Px → \(Q_x^d\))Does own price matter?
\(\beta_2\)\(\beta_2=0\)\(\beta_2\neq 0\) (Pz → \(Q_x^d\))Does the related good’s price matter?
\(\beta_3\)\(\beta_3=0\)\(\beta_3\neq 0\) (Y → \(Q_x^d\))Does income matter?
\(\beta_0\)\(\beta_0=0\)\(\beta_0\neq 0\)Is the intercept zero?

Decision rule: reject \(H_0\) if the p-value < α (the slide also writes ≤). α is the significance level, and can be 0.01, 0.05 or 0.10. The p-value is printed by Stata in the column P>|t|.

Case 1 · Statistically significant

Condition: p-value < α → reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0\).

“An increase of 1 Baht in \(P_x\) leads to an average change in \(Q_x^d\) by \(b_1\) units, holding other factors constant.”

Case 2 · Statistically insignificant

Condition: p-value > α → fail to reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0\).

“An increase of 1 Baht in \(P_x\) has no statistically significant effect on \(Q_x^d\), holding other factors constant.”

The same two sentences are used for \(P_z\) (with \(b_2\)) and for income \(Y\) (with \(b_3\)). The sign of \(b\) gives the direction of the change; its size is in units of quantity per 1 unit of the X variable.

12. Turning a regression coefficient into an elasticity

The slope \(b_1\) is in units (e.g. “−2.16 units per $1”), not percent. To get an elasticity, multiply by the ratio of sample means, because \(b_1=\Delta Q/\Delta P\):

\[ E_d=\frac{\%\Delta Q_x^d}{\%\Delta P_x}=\frac{\Delta Q_x^d}{\Delta P_x}\cdot\frac{\bar P_x}{\bar Q_x^d}=b_1\cdot\frac{\bar P_x}{\bar Q_x^d} \qquad E_c=b_2\cdot\frac{\bar P_z}{\bar Q_x^d} \qquad E_y=b_3\cdot\frac{\bar Y}{\bar Q_x^d} \]
Statistical prerequisiteEstimated elasticity
Reject \(H_0:\beta=0\) (significant)\(b\times\dfrac{\overline{X}}{\bar Q}\) — the formula above
Fail to reject \(H_0:\beta=0\) (insignificant)0 — the slide sets the elasticity to zero, no multiplication

Price elasticity at the sample means

Because \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) is rejected, we compute:

\[ E_d=\frac{\%\Delta Q^d_x}{\%\Delta P_x}=\frac{\Delta Q^d_x}{\Delta P_x}\cdot\frac{\bar P_x}{\bar Q^d_x}=b_1\cdot\frac{\bar P_x}{\bar Q^d_x}=-2.16\times\frac{5.5}{37.8}=-0.31 \]

Slide sentence: “If the price increases by 1%, the quantity demanded falls by 0.31%.” Since |−0.31| < 1, this demand is inelastic.

No-calculator route

37.8 ÷ 5.5 ≈ 7, so 5.5/37.8 ≈ 1/7. Then 2.16 ÷ 7 ≈ 0.31. Sign from \(b_1\): negative. Answer −0.31.

\(15.1 \div 175 = 0.0863\); \(\;12.202\times 0.0863 = 1.053\) → \(E_d\approx -1.05\). (Mental check: \(12\times15=180\) and \(180/175\) is just above 1.)

If a question quotes “−1.503” from the slide, accept it as the slide’s figure; if it asks you to compute \(-12.202\times15.1/175\), the arithmetic answer is about −1.05.

Simple vs multiple regression (slide “Elasticity using Regression”): in the simple model \(\hat Q=b_0+b_1Price\), price elasticity is \(b_1\cdot\overline{Price}/\bar Q\) and the advertising and GPP elasticities are NA (those variables are not in the model). In the multiple model, each is \(b_k\cdot\bar X_k/\bar Q\) — set to 0 if insignificant.

Recap — the 12 things to know cold

  1. Demand = willingness and ability to buy at various prices over a period. Law of demand: P↑ → Qd↓.
  2. Market demand = sum of individual quantities at each price (4 + 5 + 100 = 109 at $12).
  3. Own price → movement along the curve. Income, preference, substitute price ↑, complement price ↓ → shift right.
  4. \(E=\%\Delta Q/\%\Delta P=(\Delta Q/\Delta P)(\bar P/\bar Q)\), midpoints. Slide example = 3.67 (elastic).
  5. |E| > 1 elastic, < 1 inelastic, = 1 unit, ∞ perfectly elastic (horizontal), 0 perfectly inelastic (vertical).
  6. Elastic: TR moves opposite to P. Inelastic: TR moves with P. Unit: TR unchanged. TR max at the unit-elastic midpoint (800).
  7. Cross: + substitutes, − complements. Income: + normal (>1 luxury, <1 necessity), − inferior.
  8. β = true unknown parameter; b = sample estimate. \(H_0:\beta=0\) vs \(H_1:\beta\neq0\).
  9. Reject \(H_0\) if p < α (0.01, 0.05, 0.10). Stars: *** < 0.01, ** < 0.05, * < 0.10.
  10. Significant b: “+1 in X changes Q by b units, other factors constant.” Insignificant: “no significant effect” and elasticity = 0.
  11. Example 1: \(\hat y=49.667-2.1576x\), means 37.8 and 5.5, \(E_d=-0.31\) (inelastic), R² = 0.9757, ignore intercept.
  12. Example 2: only price significant (p = 0.037, **): −12,200 units per $1; ads (0.971) and GPP (0.429) elasticities = 0; price elasticity printed −1.503, arithmetic ≈ −1.05 → elastic either way.
